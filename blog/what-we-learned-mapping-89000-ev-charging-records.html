What We Learned Mapping 89,000+ EV Charging Records

We set out to answer a simple question: how good is EV charging infrastructure, really, once you look past the marketing map showing thousands of pins? The answer required pulling together more than 89,000 individual charging records from a public API and building an infrastructure-quality score from data that was, to put it mildly, not designed to be analyzed this way.

The Problem With "Number of Stations" as a Metric

Most public EV infrastructure conversations center on a single headline number — how many charging stations exist in a state or city. That number is close to meaningless on its own. A station that's been offline for six months still counts. A station with one working plug counts the same as one with eight. Raw station counts flatter regions that look good on a map and hide the ones that don't actually work when a driver needs them.

"Raw station counts flatter regions that look good on a map and hide the ones that don't actually work when a driver needs them."

What the Raw Data Actually Looked Like

  • Inconsistent status fields — "operational," "available," and blank entries were sometimes used interchangeably for the same real-world condition.
  • Duplicate listings for the same physical location under slightly different names or coordinates.
  • Missing connector-type data for a meaningful share of records, making reliability comparisons across networks unreliable without cleanup.
  • Update timestamps that ranged from real-time to multiple years stale, with no consistent way to tell which was which at a glance.

Building a Score That Reflects Reality, Not Just Volume

Instead of counting stations, we weighted the dataset by working connector density, reported uptime signals, and network diversity per region — essentially asking "if you needed to charge right now in this area, how likely is that to actually work," not "how many pins are on the map." That reframing changed the ranking meaningfully: several regions with high station counts scored lower once non-functional or duplicate listings were filtered out, while some lower-count regions scored higher because their infrastructure was consistently reliable.

Why This Kind of Work Matters Beyond EVs

The pattern here isn't specific to charging stations. Any business or public dataset built from third-party or crowd-sourced inputs tends to carry the same issues — duplicates, inconsistent status fields, and stale records that inflate a headline number. The lesson generalizes: before trusting any "total count" metric from a public or aggregated data source, it's worth asking what's actually being measured underneath it, and whether stale or duplicate entries are quietly padding the number.

What This Project Reinforced About Working With Messy Data

  • Clean your join keys first — duplicate location matching was the single biggest source of inflated counts.
  • Treat "last updated" timestamps as a data quality signal in their own right, not just metadata to ignore.
  • A weighted quality score almost always tells a more useful story than a raw count, especially with public infrastructure data.
  • Document your cleaning assumptions — anyone using an infrastructure score to make a real decision needs to know what was filtered out and why.

FAQ

Why isn't the total number of EV charging stations a reliable metric?

Raw station counts include offline, duplicate, and non-functional listings, which can significantly overstate real-world availability in a given region.

What makes public EV charging data hard to analyze?

Common issues include inconsistent status labeling, duplicate location entries, missing connector-type information, and update timestamps that range from real-time to years out of date.

How can businesses build a more accurate infrastructure quality score?

Weight the data by functional connector density, reliability signals, and network diversity rather than raw counts, and clean duplicate and stale entries before drawing conclusions.
